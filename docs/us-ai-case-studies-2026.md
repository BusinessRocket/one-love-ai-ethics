# U.S. AI Case Studies, 2025–2026

Ten current American cases and laws, each read through a contemplative lens. Facts are summarised in our own words from the sources listed at the end; status is as of **October 2026** and may have changed since.

> Several cases involve suicide. If you are struggling, please reach out: Lifeline **13 11 14** (Australia), **988** (United States), or your local crisis line.

---

## 1. Garcia v. Character Technologies — chatbots and a child's life

**What happened.** A mother in Florida sued Character Technologies (Character.AI) and others after her teenage son died by suicide following months of intense conversations with role-play chatbots. In May 2025 a federal judge in Orlando let most claims go forward and declined, at that early stage, to treat the chatbot's output as protected speech that would end the case. The family settled and the case was dismissed in January 2026.

**Principle.** *Ahiṃsā* — non-harm, and *heyaṃ duḥkham anāgatam*: prevent the suffering not yet come (YS 2.16).

**Discuss.** What safeguards should exist before a product designed to feel like a companion is offered to minors? Who is responsible when a persona built by users causes harm?

## 2. Raine v. OpenAI — duty of care in a general chatbot

**What happened.** In August 2025 the parents of 16-year-old Adam Raine filed a wrongful-death suit in San Francisco Superior Court, alleging ChatGPT contributed to his suicide. Claims include design defect, failure to warn and negligence. Further suits against chatbot makers followed in 2025–2026.

**Principle.** *Right speech*: true, beneficial, timely and kind. A system that only mirrors a user back to themselves can fail the "beneficial" test.

**Discuss.** When should a chatbot stop the conversation and connect a person to human help? How should long conversations be monitored without violating privacy?

## 3. FTC 6(b) inquiry into AI companions

**What happened.** On 11 September 2025 the Federal Trade Commission voted 3–0 to send study orders to seven companies offering consumer AI companion chatbots. It asked about safety testing, age limits and parental controls, how engagement is monetised, and how complaints are handled — with special focus on children and teens.

**Principle.** *Brahmacarya* — moderation and the right use of energy, including attention. Business models built on maximum engagement pull against wellbeing.

**Discuss.** Is it ethical to design an AI that users feel they cannot put down?

## 4. California SB 243 — the first companion-chatbot law

**What happened.** From 1 January 2026, operators of companion chatbots in California must disclose that the bot is AI where users could be misled; publish protocols that refer users expressing suicidal thoughts to crisis services; remind known minors at least every three hours that the bot is not human and to take a break; block sexually explicit content for minors; and from July 2027 report crisis-referral data. Users can sue for violations.

**Principle.** *Satya* — truthfulness. The law turns "don't pretend to be human" into a legal duty.

**Discuss.** Should these protections cover adults too?

## 5. Bartz v. Anthropic — the largest copyright settlement

**What happened.** Authors sued Anthropic over books used to train its Claude models. In June 2025 Judge William Alsup ruled that training on lawfully purchased books could be fair use, but keeping a central library of pirated copies was not. Anthropic agreed to pay $1.5 billion — roughly $3,000 per book across more than 400,000 works — and Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín granted final approval on 20 July 2026.

*Disclosure: this repository was drafted with the help of Claude, which is made by Anthropic. The case is included because it is the largest copyright recovery in U.S. history, not to favour or criticise any company.*

**Principle.** *Asteya* — non-stealing. How data is obtained matters as much as what it is used for.

**Discuss.** Is "it was available online" ever a sufficient ethical reason to train on someone's work?

## 6. Thaler v. Perlmutter — no copyright for AI as author

**What happened.** Stephen Thaler sought copyright for an image he said was created autonomously by his AI system. The Copyright Office refused for lack of a human author; the D.C. Circuit upheld that in March 2025, and the Supreme Court declined to hear the case on 2 March 2026.

**Principle.** *Asmitā* — the I-am-ness that Patanjali lists as an affliction (YS 2.3). Who is the "self" that creates? The law, for now, says it must be human.

**Discuss.** How much human input should be enough for a work made with AI tools to belong to its human creator?

## 7. The New York Times v. OpenAI and Microsoft — privacy caught in the crossfire

**What happened.** The Times sued OpenAI and Microsoft for training on its journalism. Its case and many others are consolidated before Judge Sidney Stein in New York. In January 2026 the court ordered OpenAI to produce 20 million anonymised ChatGPT conversation logs as evidence.

**Principle.** *Aparigraha* — non-possessiveness. Data that is kept can be demanded. Collecting less is the surest protection.

**Discuss.** Would you speak to an AI about your spiritual life or mental health if you knew the logs might end up in a lawsuit?

## 8. Mobley v. Workday — bias in AI hiring

**What happened.** Derek Mobley alleges that Workday's AI screening tools disproportionately rejected older, minority and disabled applicants. Judge Rita Lin in the Northern District of California dismissed intentional-discrimination claims but kept disparate-impact claims, and in 2025 let the age claims proceed as a nationwide collective action. As of August 2026 a class-certification hearing was scheduled. Workday denies the claims. Separately, Illinois HB 3773 (in force 1 January 2026) bars employers from using ZIP codes as a proxy in AI hiring.

**Principle.** *Equanimity (upekṣā)* in its true sense — not indifference, but even-handedness toward all people.

**Discuss.** If a model was never told anyone's age or race, can it still discriminate? How would you test for it?

## 9. Federal vs. state — who writes the rules?

**What happened.** An executive order of 11 December 2025 declared a policy of "minimally burdensome" national AI standards and set up a Justice Department AI Litigation Task Force to challenge state laws. In April 2026 the Justice Department joined a challenge to Colorado's AI Act; Colorado's attorney general agreed not to enforce it, and on 14 May 2026 Governor Polis signed a narrower replacement (SB 26-189). Meanwhile state laws took effect on 1 January 2026, including California's SB 53 (transparency and incident reporting for frontier AI developers) and SB 243, Texas's Responsible AI Governance Act, and Illinois HB 3773.

**Principle.** *The Middle Way* — between a patchwork of fifty rulebooks and a single light-touch standard that may leave harms unaddressed.

**Discuss.** Which decisions about AI should be made nationally, and which locally?

## 10. TAKE IT DOWN Act — deepfakes and dignity

**What happened.** Signed in May 2025, the law criminalises publishing non-consensual intimate images, including AI-generated deepfakes, and requires covered platforms to remove reported images within 48 hours of a valid request. The FTC began enforcing the platform duties on 19 May 2026 and opened a complaint site for victims.

**Principle.** *Ahiṃsā* and *śauca* (purity) — protecting the dignity of the body and the person.

**Discuss.** Is 48 hours fast enough? What should happen to the tools that make such images?

## Pattern across all ten

| Pattern | Cases |
|---|---|
| Harm to minors and people in crisis is driving the fastest legal change | 1, 2, 3, 4 |
| How training data is obtained now carries billion-dollar consequences | 5, 7 |
| Humans remain the legal authors and the accountable parties | 6, 8 |
| Data kept is data that can be exposed | 7 |
| Rules are being contested between federal and state governments | 9 |
| Dignity and consent are being written into law | 4, 10 |
