# Meditation: Dos and Avoids

A survey of what four great teachers — Patanjali, the Buddha, Padmasambhava and Milarepa — advise meditators to cultivate and to avoid, followed by what modern research adds, and a short self-check survey members can answer.

> This is educational material, not medical advice and not a substitute for a qualified teacher. Where a tradition says a practice needs transmission, we respect that and do not give the instructions here.

---

## 1. Where all four agree

Before the differences, the common ground. Every one of these teachers puts the same things at the base of practice:

| Shared principle | Patanjali | The Buddha | Padmasambhava | Milarepa |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ethics first** | Yamas and niyamas are limbs 1 and 2 (YS 2.29) | Sīla is the first of the three trainings | View vast as the sky, conduct fine as flour | Spent his life purifying past harm |
| **Steady practice** | Long, unbroken, wholehearted practice (YS 1.14) | Right effort | Retreat practice and recitation | Years of cave practice |
| **Non-attachment** | Vairāgya (YS 1.15) | Letting go of craving (2nd Noble Truth) | Recognising experiences as empty | Songs on the futility of worldly ambition |
| **Don't chase powers** | Siddhis are obstacles (YS 3.37) | Psychic powers not to be displayed for show | Experiences (nyams) are passing mist | Realisation, not display, is the point |
| **The four attitudes** | Friendliness, compassion, gladness, equanimity (YS 1.33) | The four brahmavihāras | Compassion as the basis of all Vajrayana | Compassion for those who harmed him |
| **A real teacher** | Īśvara as the first teacher (YS 1.26) | Spiritual friendship is "the whole of the holy life" | Empowerment, transmission, instruction | Total reliance on Marpa |

## 2. Patanjali — the Yoga Sutras

**Do**
- Sit in a posture that is **steady and comfortable** (sthira-sukha, 2.46). Comfort is part of the instruction, not a weakness.
- Pair **practice with non-attachment** (1.12): show up daily, hold results lightly.
- Choose **one object** of focus when the mind is scattered (1.32).
- Meet others with **friendliness, compassion, gladness and equanimity** (1.33).
- When a harmful thought arises, **cultivate its opposite** (pratipakṣa-bhāvana, 2.33).
- Let the breath become **long and subtle** through observation (2.50), not force.
- Practise **self-study** (svādhyāya) and **dedication** (īśvara-praṇidhāna) as part of daily life (2.1).

**Avoid**
- Skipping the ethical limbs and going straight to "advanced" practice.
- Chasing **siddhis** or special experiences (3.37).
- Letting the nine obstacles become a story about yourself: illness, dullness, doubt, carelessness, laziness, indulgence, false perception, not progressing, losing progress (1.30). They are normal; name them and continue.
- Forceful breathing presented as "Patanjali's prāṇāyāma" — the Sutras do not teach it.

## 3. The Buddha — Pāli discourses and early tradition

**Do**
- Begin with **mindfulness of breathing**: simply know the in-breath and out-breath.
- Use the **four foundations** — body, feelings, mind, mental phenomena — as your map.
- Balance **calm (samatha) and insight (vipassanā)**.
- Tune effort like **Soṇa's lute**: not too tight, not too loose.
- Recognise and name the **five hindrances**; apply the matching antidote.
- Cultivate **loving-kindness** in widening circles, including yourself.
- Test teachings in your own experience, as the **Kālāma Sutta** advises.

**Avoid**
- The two extremes: **indulgence and self-torment** (the Middle Way).
- **Clinging to views**, including Buddhist ones — the raft is for crossing, not carrying.
- Comparing your attainments with others or advertising them.
- Using meditation to escape ethics: the precepts come first.

## 4. Padmasambhava — Vajrayana and the Nyingma tradition

**Do**
- Hold **vast view and careful conduct** together.
- Recite the **Vajra Guru mantra** with devotion if it inspires you — this is open to all.
- **Receive empowerment, transmission and instruction** from a qualified lama before deity, channel or breath yogas.
- Keep **samaya** (commitments) and respect the teacher–student bond.
- Treat bliss, clarity and thought-free states as **nyams** — passing experiences.
- Visit sacred places such as Taktsang with **respect and simplicity**, not as trophies.

**Avoid**
- **Self-initiation** from books, apps or videos into restricted practices.
- **Spiritual pride**: using "high view" to excuse harmful behaviour.
- Treating sacred biography as either literal history or worthless myth — hold it with respect.
- Commercialising mantras, empowerments or relics.

## 5. Milarepa — the Kagyu tradition

**Do**
- Let **remorse become motivation** to change, not a reason for despair.
- **Persevere** through difficulty — real change takes years.
- Live **simply**; reduce what distracts you from practice.
- Practise **devotion and trust** in a teacher you have examined carefully.
- Learn **Mahāmudrā** from a qualified teacher when ready.
- Read his **songs** — they teach through joy, humour and nature, not only hardship.

**Avoid**
- **Imitating his austerities**: starvation, extreme isolation, cold exposure.
- Practising **tummo** or similar breath-and-heat methods without empowerment and guidance.
- **Hyperventilation or breath-holding near water** or while driving — fainting can be fatal.
- Accepting **abusive treatment** from a teacher because "Marpa was harsh with Milarepa". Tradition presents that relationship as unique; modern communities must protect students.

## 6. What modern research adds

Meditation is beneficial for many people, but it is not risk-free.

- A 2020 systematic review in *Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica* (83 studies, 6,703 participants) found adverse events in about **8.3%** of participants, most commonly anxiety, depression and unusual cognitive experiences.
- A 2021 study led by Willoughby Britton at Brown University (96 people in mindfulness-based programmes) found **58%** reported at least one meditation-related adverse effect, **37%** some negative impact on functioning, and **6%** lasting bad effects beyond one month.

**Practical safety rules from this evidence**

| Do | Avoid |
|---|---|
| Start with 5–20 minutes a day | Jumping straight into long silent retreats |
| Tell a teacher about trauma, psychosis, bipolar disorder, seizures, pregnancy or heart conditions | Hiding health history from retreat staff |
| Use eyes-open and grounding practices if you feel overwhelmed | Pushing through panic, dissociation or terror |
| Keep regular sleep and meals | Sleep deprivation or extreme fasting as a "shortcut" |
| Stop or shorten practice when it consistently feels worse | Treating stopping as failure |
| See a doctor or mental-health professional if symptoms persist | Replacing therapy or medication with meditation |

**If you are in crisis**, contact emergency services or a crisis line: Lifeline **13 11 14** (Australia), **988** (United States), or your local equivalent.

## 7. Self-check survey for members

Answer privately, or share as much as you like through the **Practice survey** issue form in this repository. There are no right answers; this is svādhyāya — self-study.

1. Which tradition or teacher do you mainly practise with, if any?
2. How many minutes a day do you meditate, on average?
3. What is your main anchor or method (breath, mantra, mettā, open awareness, other)?
4. Do you have a teacher or community you can ask questions?
5. Which of the five hindrances visits you most often?
6. Has meditation ever made you feel worse? What helped?
7. Which ethical commitment (a yama or precept) are you working on now?
8. Have you ever pushed beyond "steady and comfortable"? What happened?
9. Is there a practice you are drawn to that the tradition says needs transmission?
10. How has practice changed the way you treat other people?
11. What would you like an AI to **never** do when people ask about meditation?
12. One line of intention you would like to share with the circle.
